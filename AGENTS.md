# Repository instructions

## Investment valuation requirements confirmed by the user (2026-11-20)

Apply these requirements to future investment research and revisions, including industry, sector, subsector, company, and ETF comparisons:

- Deliver empirical valuation analysis, not merely explanations of valuation theory or a list of candidate metrics. Calculate actual current values, make comparisons, and state what the current price implies. Put findings and evidence before methodology; move lengthy theory to an appendix.
- Select a primary valuation metric and independent cross-checks for each business model, lifecycle stage, and current cycle. Do not default every sector to PE or PB. Explain why the chosen metric is appropriate now, how it connects operating drivers to sustainable cash flows and equity or enterprise value, what accounting distortions it addresses, and when it fails.
- Address the user's request for the indicator most closely related to price or value with evidence. Separate economic relevance, explanation of current market pricing, and prediction of future total returns. Do not claim a metric has the highest correlation without a relevant, reproducible empirical comparison; price-containing multiples have mechanical price correlation. State when empirical superiority remains unverified.
- Calculate current historical valuation percentiles for comparable 10-, 5-, and 3-year windows where supported, with actual charts, dates, frequency, valid sample counts, percentile direction, and reference levels. Distinguish price percentile or drawdown from valuation percentile and intrinsic-value discount. Label partial histories honestly; never silently drop losses, splice incompatible definitions, or fabricate missing values.
- Calculate overseas mature-market comparisons using appropriately matched business and lifecycle peers. Show actual peer values, median/range, sample size, the researched asset's position within that specified cross-section, and premium/discount. Where supported, also calculate the historical position of the domestic/overseas relative valuation ratio; distinguish this from each market's own historical percentile. Do not call a tiny peer sample a whole-market percentile.
- Examine growth, margins, ROIC/ROE, patent duration, reinvestment, leverage, country risk, tax, lease treatment, R&D capitalization, interest cash-flow classification, reporting periods, and currency/share-class definitions before interpreting international valuation gaps. Report raw comparisons and any supported adjustments separately. Do not manufacture comparability where no direct peers exist, such as treating overseas consumer health as a global traditional Chinese medicine sector.
- Provide an explicit present-tense valuation judgment for each covered segment: relatively cheap, reasonable, expensive, or indeterminate, with numerical evidence, confidence, assumptions, and contrary signals. Distinguish cheap relative to its own history from cheap in absolute cash-flow terms or versus overseas peers. Do not choose only the metric that supports a preferred conclusion.
- State the universe and coverage. Representative company baskets are not whole sectors or ETF portfolios. Calculate basket aggregates from underlying numerators and denominators with declared weights, preserve negative cash flows, disclose loss-making constituents and exclusions, and show material internal dispersion. Explain mismatches among the researched business, index, and ETF exposures, including overlap across ETFs.
- Reconcile normalized or recurring profits and cash flows with reported figures; label proxies and approximate share counts. Do not assume consolidated CFO minus capital expenditures is pure FCFE or FCFF. Retain reproducible calculations and raw public-source evidence, check arithmetic, and give access to supporting data alongside the report.
- Translate actual valuation findings into strategy and ETF judgments, and revise earlier preferences when the numbers contradict them. Set conditional entry/add/reduction/invalidation rules using supported historical levels and earnings/cash-flow scenarios. Translate to tradable price targets and cost-adjusted reward/risk only when the mapping is justified. Missing data require a concrete coverage limit and an indeterminate conclusion, not a theoretical substitute or unsupported ranking.

For industry and sector research, apply the industry research framework and include a dedicated **sector valuation and historical percentile** section. Define the sector universe, select a representative index, and explain any exposure mismatch between the index, ETF, and researched companies. Choose metrics appropriate to the business model and cycle, such as recurring PE and FCF yield for profitable equipment makers, PB/ROE for banks, normalized EV/EBITDA for cyclicals, and rNPV for pre-profit drug developers. Report the sector aggregate and methodology, including weighting, loss-making constituents, one-off gains, and index rebalances where relevant. Keep ETF prices and premium/discount separate from underlying sector valuation.

Use comparable historical data to plot 10-, 5-, and 3-year windows when supported. For shorter series, plot the entire available history and report only supported windows. Label the series, metric, dates, frequency, source, coverage, and current value. Compute current percentiles separately within each window, disclose percentile direction and sample count, and show relevant historical reference levels. Never infer a percentile from a price chart or splice incompatible valuation definitions. If data are incomplete, state the verified coverage and missing windows. Interpret valuation alongside normalized earnings, growth, margins, and cash flow. Use historical levels and earnings scenarios to set conditional entry, add, reduction, and invalidation rules for sector exposure. Translate those rules to stock and ETF price plans only when the mapping is supported, and report cost-adjusted reward/risk.
